Giving Thanks for Great Vendor Partnerships
 
 

What makes a vendor worth keeping, and how boards and residents can work with them

Most of the work that keeps a community running is done by vendors: the people who repair, clean, paint, maintain and respond. In 2026, MCM communities completed 655 projects with the help of 523 vendors. Every one of them improved everyday life for residents, and every one happened because a vendor showed up and did the work.

Featured Projects
Water and irrigation
Pump repairs, sprinkler system maintenance, backflow testing
 
Roofs and drainage
Roof leak repairs, gutter and downspout cleaning
 
Gates, fences and structures
Gate repairs, fence installations, balcony restoration
 
Elevators
Elevator door maintenance
 
Sometimes It's Nobody's Fault

Not every project goes smoothly, and the reason isn't always a mistake. Materials arrive late. Weather interrupts the schedule. Once a wall or a deck is opened up, the damage turns out to be bigger than expected. Permits take longer than planned. When the situation itself is the problem, blaming the vendor, the board or the residents doesn't fix it.
 

What Makes a Great Vendor Partner

1
 Responsiveness: Answers calls and requests promptly, and shows up when they say they will.
2
 Clear communication: Explains scope, cost and timing up front, and flags changes before they happen.
3
 Quality work: Does the job right the first time, so the same repair isn't needed twice.
4
 Reliability: Follows through from start to finish, with few surprises along the way.
 
What vendors can do
Explain the scope, cost and timing up front
Give notice before work disrupts daily life
Tell the board early when plans change, and why
What boards and residents can do
Allow access and follow schedules
Be patient when the unexpected comes up
Raise concerns early and calmly
 
The Takeaway

Every project on this list happened because vendors and communities worked together. When the unexpected comes up, the communities that handle it best are the ones where both sides understand what the other needs. That is something to be thankful for.

The Boardroom

Budget Season: Who's at the Table?
 
 

Why the vendor list draws the most debate, and how to approach it

As the year winds down, many boards are finalizing next year's budget. In our experience, no line of that budget draws more discussion than the vendor list. Vendors touch almost every part of community life, so decisions about who to keep, who to replace and what to pay them tend to be the most argued.

Why the Vendor List Is Debated
Price vs performance:
The lowest bid isn't always the best value over a full year.
Loyalty vs fresh bids:
Some members want to stay with familiar vendors, while others want to see what else is out there.
Visibility:
Residents see vendors' work every day, so opinions come easily.
 
How to Approach Budgeting and Vendor Selection
✓
 Start with this year's actuals: A budget committee typically starts with the current year's budget and reviews projected variances before adjusting each line.
✓
 Fund the reserves: The annual budget report must include a summary of the association's reserves, so reserve contributions belong in the budget from the start.
✓
 Compare bids on scope, not just price: For larger contracts, get multiple bids and make sure each covers the same work.
✓
 Judge vendors on more than cost: Look at licensing, insurance, references, responsiveness and how clearly they communicate.
✓
 Document the decision: Record why a vendor was chosen in the meeting minutes and keep contracts organized, so the reasoning is clear when board members change.
 
The Takeaway

The best vendor lists are built on performance and communication, not just the lowest number. Good vendors are worth being thankful for, and worth budgeting for.

Seasonal Checklist

Checked, Cleared and Ready for Thanksgiving
 
 

Your HOA's Thanksgiving and early winter checklist

With Thanksgiving just around the corner and the rainy season ahead, now is a good time for a few simple checks. They help your community host holiday guests comfortably and head into winter prepared.

✓
 Clear fallen leaves and debris from gutters, downspouts, walkways and storm drains before the rain arrives.
✓
 Check roofs, flashing and the caulking around balcony doors and windows for gaps where water could get in.
✓
 Check outdoor lighting at walkways, entrances and parking areas now that the days are shorter.
✓
 Review guest parking rules, signage and any clubhouse reservation requirements before holiday gatherings.
✓
 Make sure balconies and railings are in good condition before hosting.
✓
 Remind residents to test smoke and carbon monoxide alarms and keep exits clear when cooking and hosting.
✓
 Review community rules on holiday decorations, including when they go up and when they come down.

A little preparation now keeps the holidays, and the rainy season, running smoothly.

Improved Balconies, Just in Time for Thanksgiving
 
 
Project Spotlight
Dessert on the Balcony This Thanksgiving
Unit E  Unit F

With Thanksgiving approaching, two repaired balconies are ready to use again, whether for a quiet moment outside or a slice of pie after dinner.

The inspection: A safety inspection under SB 326, the California law that requires condo associations to have balconies, decks, stairs and railings inspected by a licensed architect or structural engineer every nine years, found serious wood decay in the decks and framing of Units E and F. The inspector rated them as the most urgent, life-safety category and recommended restricting access right away.

The fix: The old wood decking was removed and replaced with composite decking. Deteriorated joists were repaired or replaced, waterproof strips were added to protect the framing, damaged fascia was replaced, and the guardrails were replaced with code-compliant ones. Unit E's top deck also had a gap where the deck meets the wall, which was sealed and flashed to keep water out of the building's framing.

The result is a safer, more durable balcony that residents can enjoy through the holidays and beyond.

It's a practical improvement with lasting benefits, and a pleasant spot for dessert after the big meal.

Quick Knowledge

California HOA Facts & Myths: Vendor Edition
 
 

Think you know how HOAs work with vendors? Let's separate fact from fiction.

Myth 1
“The board has to accept the lowest bid.”
Fact
Boards are not required to accept the lowest bid, and the lowest bid is not always the best bid. Contracts are generally awarded to the best bidder, as determined by the board. A low bid can leave out work that costs more later.
 
Myth 2
“Homeowners can review every bid and contract.”
Fact
Members can inspect executed contracts, but not proposals or bids. Bids and contract proposals are among the records that members cannot inspect.
 
Myth 3
“Any contractor can work on the common areas.”
Fact
Vendors who work in or on the common areas must be licensed and insured. Boards should verify each contractor's license with the Contractors State License Board and get proof of insurance.
 
Myth 4
“Any board member can give a vendor instructions.”
Fact
Individual directors do not have the right to contact vendors, give them instructions or sign contracts without board authorization. Decisions go through the board, which keeps requests clear for the vendor.
 
The Takeaway

Knowing how vendors are chosen, hired and directed helps owners and boards trust the process, and the vendors doing the work.

Ask MCM

Keeping the Vendors Worth Keeping
 
 
Q
 Does an HOA have to rebid its vendors every year?
A
No, but contracts should be reviewed regularly.

If an association already has a good company providing good services, the board is not obligated to require it to rebid its services annually. Forcing an annual bidding cycle can lead a vendor to see the relationship as short-term. Keeping a reliable vendor also saves the time and risk of onboarding a new one.
 
1 year
Many CC&Rs limit association contracts to one year
  
3–5 years
Some communities amend their CC&Rs to allow longer terms for certain vendors
 

Contract terms matter as much as bidding. An evergreen clause renews a contract automatically unless the board gives the vendor written notice that it won't renew. Noting renewal dates ahead of time keeps the board in control of the decision.

The MCM Takeaway

Review vendor performance and contract terms every year, and check your governing documents for contract limits. Rebid when service slips or costs rise, not just because a year has passed.
